Why Your Excel Files Are Costing Your Business More Than You Think

January 2026·6 min read·Selixor Systems

This is not an argument against spreadsheets. Excel and Google Sheets are powerful tools — and in many contexts, the right tool. This is an argument against using them as an operational system when the volume, complexity, and decision-making requirements of a business have outgrown what manual spreadsheets can reliably support.

And most businesses cross that threshold much earlier than they realize.

Data point: A 2024 study by Gartner found that data professionals spend an average of 44% of their working time on data preparation and manual reporting — not on analysis or decision-making. That is nearly half of every working day spent on tasks that can be automated.

But the Real Cost Is Not Time

The time cost is significant. But the more damaging cost of manual spreadsheet dependency is something harder to quantify: decision latency.

When data is compiled manually once a week or once a month, decisions are made on information that is already outdated by the time it reaches the decision-maker. In fast-moving markets — retail, food service, logistics, e-commerce — a 30-day information lag can mean the difference between catching a problem early and discovering it after significant damage has been done.

One retail client we worked with discovered — through structured data analysis — that a product category had been generating negative margins for 4 months. The data existed in their POS system the entire time. It simply wasn't structured to be visible.

The 4 Failure Modes of Spreadsheet-Based Operations

1. Version proliferation

When multiple people maintain separate spreadsheets that are periodically merged, data inconsistencies are inevitable. Research by the University of Hawaii found that 88% of spreadsheets contain errors — and in operational contexts, those errors translate directly into flawed decisions.

2. Formula fragility

Complex spreadsheet formulas built over time by one person become invisible infrastructure. When that person leaves, or when the spreadsheet is modified by someone else, formulas break silently — producing incorrect outputs that look correct until a major discrepancy surfaces.

3. Scaling failure

Spreadsheets that work well at 500 rows begin to degrade at 5,000 rows and become unworkable at 50,000. Businesses that experience growth often don't realize their reporting infrastructure has a hard ceiling until they've already hit it.

4. Audit impossibility

When a number in a spreadsheet report is questioned, tracing it back to its source data often requires hours of investigation across multiple files. In regulated industries or during due diligence processes, this creates significant operational risk.
